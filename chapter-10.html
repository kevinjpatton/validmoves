Predicate Logic: Derivations

SD plus four quantifier rules gives PD. The interesting part is the restrictions on ∀I and ∃E, which make sure the instantiating constant really is arbitrary.

Section 10.1

The derivation system PD

pp. 474–491 · exercises 10.1E

PD keeps every rule of SD and adds an elimination and introduction rule for each quantifier. Each involves a quantified sentence and a substitution instance of it; the constant used is the instantiating constant.

  • ∀E: from (∀x)P, enter any instance P(a/x). Let the goal tell you which constant to pick.
  • ∃I: from an instance P(a/x), enter (∃x)P. Since P(a/x) replaces every x, from Laa you can reach (∃x)Lxa, (∃x)Lax or (∃x)Lxx.
  • ∀I: from P(a/x), enter (∀x)P, provided (i) a occurs in no open assumption and (ii) a doesn't occur in (∀x)P.
  • ∃E: from (∃x)P and a subderivation assuming P(a/x) and ending in Q, enter Q, provided (i) a occurs in no open assumption, (ii) a doesn't occur in (∃x)P, and (iii) a doesn't occur in Q.

The restrictions all express one idea: when you reason about a, nothing you've assumed may single a out. Then what holds of a holds of anything.

What each restriction blocks

∀I (i): from Fb as an assumption, infer (∀x)FxOne thing being F doesn't make everything F.
∀I (ii): from (∀x)Lxh get Lhh, then (∀x)LxxEveryone loving h doesn't mean everyone loves themselves.
∃E (iii): from (∃z)Fz, assume Fb and conclude FbSomething being F doesn't make b F.
∃E (i): the witness gets a constant already in an open assumptionThat would identify the witness with something we've assumed facts about.
Section 10.2

Syntactic properties in PD, and strategy

pp. 492–520 · exercises 10.2E

Derivability, validity, theorems, equivalence and inconsistency in PD are defined exactly as in SD. A derivation establishes them; Chapter 11 proves PD is sound and complete.

Strategies for quantifiers

  • Goal (∀x)P: derive an instance P(a/x) with a fresh constant, then ∀I. Choose a constant that appears in no open assumption and not in the goal.
  • Goal (∃x)P: derive an instance, then ∃I.
  • Accessible (∃x)P: start ∃E early. Assume an instance with a brand-new constant and aim for the goal itself inside the subderivation.
  • Accessible (∀x)P: use ∀E with whatever constants the goal and other lines need.
  • Negated quantifiers usually call for ~I or ~E, or in PD+, QN.

A worked shape: ∃ to ∃

1 | (∀x)(Fx ⊃ Gx)   Assumption
2 | (∃x)Fx          Assumption
3 | | Fa            A / ∃E
4 | | Fa ⊃ Ga       1 ∀E
5 | | Ga            3, 4 ⊃E
6 | | (∃x)Gx        5 ∃I
7 | (∃x)Gx          2, 3–6 ∃E

a is new at line 3 and gone by line 7, so all three ∃E restrictions hold.

Section 10.3

The derivation system PD+

pp. 521–525 · exercises 10.3E

PD+ adds SD+'s rules (MT, HS, DS and the replacement rules) and one new replacement rule, Quantifier Negation. Replacement rules apply to subformulas too, even open ones inside a quantifier, so it matters to identify subformulas correctly: (∀x)[Lx ⊃ (∀y)(Bxy ⊃ Jxy)] is not of the form P ⊃ (Q ⊃ R) inside.

PD is the elegant system; PD+ is the practical one. They prove the same things.

Quantifier Negation (QN)

~ (∀x)P ◁▷ (∃x)~ P
~ (∃x)P ◁▷ (∀x)~ P
Section 10.4

The derivation system PDE

pp. 526–544 · exercises 10.4E

PDE adds identity rules and lets ∀E and ∃I use any closed term, including complex terms like f(a) or g(a,b).

  • =I: enter (∀x)x = x on any line, citing nothing. It isn't an assumption; it's true on every interpretation. Then ∀E gives a = a.
  • =E: from t1 = t2 and a sentence P, enter P with one or more occurrences of t1 replaced by t2, or the other way round.

These rules handle definite descriptions: from "the Roman general who defeated Pompey conquered Gaul" and "Caesar is a Roman general who defeated Pompey", PDE derives "Caesar conquered Gaul".

Symmetry of identity

1 | | a = b          A / ⊃I
2 | | (∀x)x = x      =I
3 | | a = a          2 ∀E
4 | | b = a          1, 3 =E
5 | a = b ⊃ b = a    1–4 ⊃I

Line 4 replaces only the first a in line 3. Two ∀I steps then give (∀x)(∀y)(x = y ⊃ y = x).

Discussion questions

  1. For each restriction on ∀I and ∃E, build an invalid "derivation" that the restriction blocks.
  2. Why can ∀E and ∃I instantiate with any constant at all, while ∀I and ∃E can't?
  3. Derive (∀x)Fx ⊃ (∃x)Fx. Which assumption of the semantics (Chapter 8) does this theorem depend on?
  4. =I enters a sentence citing nothing. How is that different from an assumption?
  5. Try to derive (∃x)(∀y)Lxy from (∀y)(∃x)Lxy. Where exactly does it break?
